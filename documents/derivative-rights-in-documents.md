This page focuses solely on the rights to make derivatives of IETF Documents (RFCs and I-Ds), not the etiquette/rules relating to authors or the process around it.

## The tl;dr
All IETF Documents published since RFC 1602 came into effect on 1 April 1994 have come with a grant of rights to allow derivatives as part of the Internet Standards Process, unless they were published with a "no derivatives" clause.  No permission is required from any author. The "no derivatives" clause was first introduced in February 2004 by RFC 3667.

RFC 5378 added a new right, that of allowing derivatives **outside** of the Internet Standards Process, but had no effect on derivatives **within** the Internet Standards Process.

## The full policy timeline

### RFC 1602 introduces "contributions" and derivative rights

RFC 1602 "The Internet Standards Process -- Revision 2", which came into effect from 1 April 1994, defined a "contribution" as:
```
 o    "Contribution" means any disclosure of information or ideas,
      whether in oral, written, or other form of expression, by an
      individual or entity ("Contributor").
```
It then went on in Section 5.4.1 to set out the grant of rights, including derivative rights:

```
l.   Contributor agrees to grant, and does grant to ISOC, a
     perpetual, non-exclusive, royalty-free, world-wide right
     and license under any copyrights in the contribution to
     reproduce, distribute, perform or display publicly and
     prepare derivative works that are based on or incorporate
     all or part of the contribution, and to reproduce,
     distribute and perform or display publicly any such
     derivative works, in any form and in all languages, and to
     authorize others to do so.
```
### RFC 2026 restates that

RFC 2026 "The Internet Standards Process -- Revision 3", which came into effect from October 1996, restates this in Section 10.3.1:
```
This license
includes the right to copy, publish and distribute the
contribution in any way, and to prepare derivative works that are
based on or incorporate all or part of the contribution, the
license to such derivative works to be of the same scope as the
license of the original contribution.
```

### RFC 3667 introduces the no-derivatives clause
RFC 3667 "IETF Rights in Contributions", which came into effect from February 2004, gives a detailed exposition of why derivative rights are necessary in Section 7.3 (not reproduced here) and why limitations might necessary. It restates the derivative rights grant in Section 3.3, introducing the "no derivatives" clause:
```
 (C)  unless explicitly disallowed in the notices contained in a
      Contribution [as per Section 5.2 below], to prepare
      derivative works (other than translations) that are based on
      or incorporate all or part of the Contribution, or comment
      upon it, within the IETF Standards Process.  The license to
      such derivative works not granting the ISOC and the IETF any
      more rights than the license to the original Contribution,
```
Section 5.2 sets out the two "no derivatives" clauses that can be applied:
```
   a. "This document may not be modified, and derivative works of it may
      not be created, except to publish it as an RFC and to translate it
      into languages other than English."

   b. "This document may not be modified, and derivative works of it may
      not be created."
```
### RFC 3978 restates that unchanged
RFC 3978 "IETF Rights in Contributions", which came into effect from March 2005, is substantially the same document as RFC 3667 and restates the same exposition, rights and limitations unchanged (as they refer to derivatives).

### RFC 5377 and RFC 5378 expand derivatives to outside of the standards process
RFC 5378 "IETF Rights in Contributions", which came into effect from November 2008, allows for derivative rights without restricting it to the Internet Standards Process in Section 5.3:
```
   c. to modify or prepare derivative works (in addition to
      translations) that are based on or incorporate all or part of the
      Contribution, and to copy, publish, display, and distribute such
      derivative works, or portions thereof unless explicitly disallowed
      in the notices contained in a Contribution (in the form specified
      by the Legend Instructions), and
```
However, this now refers to RFC 5377 "Advice to the Trustees of the IETF Trust on Rights to Be Granted in IETF Documents", which only refers to this in the context of code in RFCs being copied and adapted by third parties.
